SDP1 Team Page, Part 2: the three weeks before the presentation

The ADALM-Pluto and the ten ESP32 boards have arrived. SDP1 ends with a literature review and a presentation, and SDP2 is where the full system gets built. This page covers only the time between now and the presentation. It is organised in three weeks, and every task says what to read, what to run, what the result must look like, and which file to put in the shared folder. The presentation at the end is assembled from those files and from nothing else. Part 1 stays the reference for the background, the figures and the glossary; this page links back to it where needed.

Task 1.1 Python and the FFT everyone, due 30 Oct

Read. PySDR chapter 1 and chapter 2. In chapter 2 look for the explanation of why a real sine wave shows two peaks in its FFT and for the paragraph on fftshift. You will need both to understand your plot.

Install. Python 3.11 or newer from python.org (on Windows tick "Add Python to PATH"), Visual Studio Code with its Python and Jupyter extensions, then in a terminal pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter.

Run. Create a notebook and run this cell as it is.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

fs = 1e6                 # sample rate, 1 million samples per second
N  = 4096                # number of samples
t  = np.arange(N) / fs   # time of each sample, in seconds
f  = 50e3                # frequency of the tone, 50 kHz

x = np.sin(2*np.pi*f*t)                  # a clean sine wave
x = x + 0.5*np.random.randn(N)           # add noise

X     = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft(x))                 # FFT, with zero frequency moved to the centre
freqs = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fftfreq(N, 1/fs))       # the frequency of each FFT bin

plt.figure(figsize=(9,3))
plt.plot(freqs/1e3, 20*np.log10(np.abs(X)))
plt.xlabel("frequency (kHz)"); plt.ylabel("magnitude (dB)"); plt.grid(True)
plt.show()

What you should see. Two sharp peaks, one at +50 kHz and one at -50 kHz, standing about 40 dB above a flat, ragged floor. The floor is the noise. The two peaks exist because a real sine wave contains both a positive and a negative frequency, which chapter 2 explains.

Then change things. Set f = 120e3 and run again; the peaks move. Set the noise factor from 0.5 to 5 and run again; the floor rises and the peaks shrink relative to it. Write two sentences in a markdown cell saying what moved and why.

Hand in. 01_notebooks/task11_<firstname>.ipynb, saved with the plots visible.

Task 1.2 IQ samples everyone, due 31 Oct

Read. PySDR chapter 3. Look for the section that explains why a complex sample carries both amplitude and phase, and the figure that shows a complex tone having one peak where a real tone had two. Then re-read the I and Q section of Part 1, with its clock-hand figure.

Run. In a new notebook, reuse fs, N, t and f from task 1.1 and run this.

x = np.exp(2j*np.pi*f*t)                                        # a complex tone: I = cos, Q = sin
x = x + 0.1*(np.random.randn(N) + 1j*np.random.randn(N))        # complex noise

fig, ax = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(9,8))
ax[0].plot(t[:200]*1e6, x.real[:200], label="I (real part)")
ax[0].plot(t[:200]*1e6, x.imag[:200], label="Q (imaginary part)")
ax[0].set_xlabel("time (µs)"); ax[0].legend()

X = np.fft.fftshift(np.fft.fft(x))
ax[1].plot(freqs/1e3, 20*np.log10(np.abs(X)))
ax[1].set_xlabel("frequency (kHz)"); ax[1].set_ylabel("dB")

ax[2].specgram(x, NFFT=256, Fs=fs, noverlap=128)
ax[2].set_xlabel("time (s)"); ax[2].set_ylabel("frequency (Hz)")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

What you should see. On top, two sine waves a quarter of a cycle apart. In the middle, one peak only, at +50 kHz, because a complex tone has a sign to its frequency. At the bottom, a spectrogram with one horizontal line at 50 kHz across the whole duration. Change f to -50e3 and the peak and the line move below zero. A real signal cannot do that; this is the whole reason radios deliver IQ.

Hand in. 01_notebooks/task12_<firstname>.ipynb.

Task 1.3 The Pluto receives two students, due 2 Nov

Read. The Pluto quick start, then PySDR chapter 5, which installs the software in the right order and receives the first samples. The Pluto covers 325 MHz to 3.8 GHz out of the box, so 2.4 GHz needs no modification; ignore the pages about extending the tuning range.

Step 1, plug it in. Connect the Pluto to the laptop with the supplied USB cable, into the middle USB connector on the Pluto, and screw one of the two small antennas onto the connector marked RX. Three things happen within a minute. A USB drive called PlutoSDR appears; open info.html on it to see the serial number and firmware version. A new network adapter appears, through which the Pluto is reachable at the address 192.168.2.1. On Windows a serial port also appears. If none of this happens on Windows, install the driver package from the Windows driver page and plug the Pluto in again. On macOS follow the macOS page, which has no driver to install but explains the network adapter.

Step 2, confirm it answers. Open http://192.168.2.1 in a browser. A page served by the Pluto itself appears. If it does not, the network adapter has not come up; unplug, wait ten seconds, plug in again, and on Windows check Device Manager for a device with a warning sign.

Step 3, install the software, in this order. First libiio, from the releases page (the .exe for Windows, the .pkg for macOS). Second, in a terminal, pip install pyadi-iio. Third, test from the terminal with iio_info -u ip:192.168.2.1; a long list of devices and channels, including ad9361-phy, means everything works. PySDR chapter 5 shows the same steps with screenshots.

Step 4, the capture script. Save this as capture.py. It is the script that will be used for the whole project, so read every line and the comment next to it.

import adi, json, datetime, time
import numpy as np

FC    = 2412e6     # centre frequency in Hz. 2412e6 is Wi-Fi channel 1. Change it if the team picks another channel.
FS    = 20e6       # sample rate, 20 million samples per second, enough for one 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel
GAIN  = 40         # receiver gain in dB, set by hand. Lower it if the signal clips, raise it if you only see noise.
BUF   = 2**22      # samples per buffer: 4,194,304 samples = 0.21 s at 20 Msps, always at least two beacons
EVERY = 15         # seconds between buffers
MINUTES = 10       # how long the recording lasts. Use 1 for the first test in task 1.3, 10 for the real captures.

BOARD = "01"       # the board under test, "01" to "10". Change before each recording.
POS   = "A"        # receiver position, "A" for the marked spot. Change only if the Pluto is moved.

sdr = adi.Pluto("ip:192.168.2.1")
sdr.sample_rate             = int(FS)
sdr.rx_rf_bandwidth         = int(FS)
sdr.rx_lo                   = int(FC)
sdr.gain_control_mode_chan0 = "manual"     # automatic gain would change from burst to burst and hide the fingerprint
sdr.rx_hardwaregain_chan0   = GAIN
sdr.rx_buffer_size          = BUF

for _ in range(3):
    sdr.rx()                               # throw away the first buffers while the radio settles

day  = datetime.date.today().strftime("%Y%m%d")
name = f"board{BOARD}_pos{POS}_day{day}"
nbuf = MINUTES*60 // EVERY                 # 40 buffers for a 10 minute recording

data  = np.zeros((nbuf, 2, BUF), dtype=np.int16)   # [buffer, I or Q, sample], 16-bit to keep files small
stamp = np.zeros(nbuf)
t0 = time.time()
for k in range(nbuf):
    x = sdr.rx()                           # one buffer of 0.21 s. Buffers are NOT contiguous in time.
    data[k, 0] = x.real; data[k, 1] = x.imag
    stamp[k] = time.time() - t0
    print(f"buffer {k+1} of {nbuf} at {stamp[k]:5.0f} s, peak amplitude {int(np.abs(x).max())}")
    while time.time() - t0 < (k+1)*EVERY:
        time.sleep(0.2)

np.save(name + ".npy", data)
np.save(name + "_times.npy", stamp)
json.dump({"board": BOARD, "position": POS, "day": day,
           "fc_Hz": FC, "fs_Hz": FS, "gain_dB": GAIN,
           "buffer_len": BUF, "n_buffers": int(nbuf), "seconds_between_buffers": EVERY,
           "note": "data[k,0] is I and data[k,1] is Q of buffer k; buffers are not contiguous"},
          open(name + ".json", "w"), indent=1)
print("saved", name)

The samples come out of the Pluto as 12-bit numbers, so the peak amplitude printed per buffer should sit somewhere between 200 and 1500. Below 50 the gain is too low. At 2047 the converter is clipping and the gain must come down. A 10 minute recording is 40 buffers and about 670 MB on disk, so the laptop used for captures needs about 8 GB free for the ten boards of one day.

Step 5, look at it. For this first test, turn on the hotspot of a phone, set to the 2.4 GHz band if the phone asks, and place the phone next to the Pluto. Set MINUTES = 1, run python capture.py, wait one minute, then in a notebook:

d = np.load("board01_posA_day20261101.npy")           # use the name the script printed

fig, ax = plt.subplots(2, 1, figsize=(10,7))
p = np.abs(x)**2
ax[0].plot(np.arange(len(p))/20e6*1e3, 10*np.log10(p + 1e-3))
ax[0].set_xlabel("time (ms)"); ax[0].set_ylabel("power (dB)")
ax[1].specgram(x, NFFT=1024, Fs=20e6, noverlap=512)

What you should see. In the power plot, a low floor with short rectangular bumps standing 20 to 40 dB above it, each a fraction of a millisecond to a few milliseconds long. In the spectrogram, those bumps appear as bright vertical stripes spanning most of the 20 MHz. Each stripe is one Wi-Fi packet. Turn the hotspot off and capture again; most stripes disappear, and whatever remains is the building's own Wi-Fi on the same channel.

If it does not work. "No contexts found" or a timeout on adi.Pluto means the Pluto is not reachable at 192.168.2.1; go back to step 2. A completely flat spectrogram with a peak amplitude under 10 means the antenna is on the TX connector instead of RX, or the gain is far too low. A spectrogram with a thick bright line exactly in the middle at all times is the Pluto's own DC offset; it is normal and the burst detector in week 2 ignores it.

4. Week 2, the first classification (4 to 10 Nov)

The aim of this week is a working chain from radio to answer, and one plot at the end of it. The chain is capture, find the bursts, measure three numbers per burst, train a classifier on one day, test it on the other day, and show the confusion matrix. To make sure the chain works before the presentation, the first classification is made easy on purpose, since each board is made to announce its own number in a way the Pluto can measure without decoding anything. The real hardware fingerprint, the carrier frequency offset, is measured on the same recordings and comes last, as far as time allows.

Task 2.1 Ten transmitters that announce who they are due 5 Nov

Read. The installation page of the Arduino core for ESP32 and the softAP and setTxPower entries of the Wi-Fi API page.

Step 1, the toolchain. Install the Arduino IDE, add the ESP32 board package as the installation page says, and install the USB serial driver that matches the small chip next to the USB connector (CP210x or CH340, links in section 2). In the IDE choose the board "ESP32 Dev Module" and the port that appears when a board is plugged in. Open File, Examples, WiFi, WiFiAccessPoint and flash it unchanged to one board to confirm the toolchain works; the serial monitor at 115200 baud prints the access point's address.

Step 2, how a board announces itself. An access point with no client connected sends only its beacon, one every 100 ms, and the beacon carries the network name. Decoding that name from raw IQ samples is a project in itself, so the trick is to make the name longer for each board. At the beacon's data rate of 1 Mbit/s every extra character makes the packet 8 microseconds longer, and the length of a burst is something the burst detector of task 2.3 measures for free. Board 01 gets a name two characters longer than the base name, board 02 four characters longer, and so on, so the ten boards produce ten burst lengths 16 microseconds apart. Channel and transmit power stay identical on all boards, so nothing about the hardware fingerprint is touched.

Step 3, the firmware. Replace the example with this sketch and save it as beacon_tx.ino.

#include <WiFi.h>

const int BOARD   = 1;     // CHANGE THIS for each board, 1 to 10, before flashing. Nothing else changes.
const int CHANNEL = 1;     // the same channel on all ten boards, chosen in task 2.2

char ssid[33];             // a Wi-Fi name can be at most 32 characters

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  int n = snprintf(ssid, sizeof(ssid), "SDP_FP_%02d", BOARD);   // SDP_FP_01 ... SDP_FP_10, 9 characters
  for (int i = 0; i < 2*BOARD; i++) ssid[n + i] = '-';           // add 2, 4, ... 20 dashes: the board's length code
  ssid[n + 2*BOARD] = 0;
  WiFi.mode(WIFI_AP);
  WiFi.softAP(ssid, nullptr, CHANNEL);                           // open network, no password, fixed channel
  WiFi.setTxPower(WIFI_POWER_19_5dBm);                           // same transmit power on every board
  Serial.print("access point started: "); Serial.println(ssid);
  Serial.print("name length: ");          Serial.println(strlen(ssid));
  Serial.print("MAC address: ");          Serial.println(WiFi.softAPmacAddress());
}

void loop() {
  delay(1000);                                                   // nothing to do; the radio sends beacons by itself
}

Task 2.3 Find the bursts and measure three numbers per burst due 7 Nov with the day 1 files, re-run on 10 Nov with day 2

What this does. A recording is mostly silence with short bursts. The first step finds where each burst starts and ends, by computing the power of each sample, smoothing it, and marking where it rises above a threshold set from the silent parts. The second step measures three numbers for each burst and writes one row per burst to a table. The three numbers are the burst length in microseconds (the board's announced number from task 2.1), the mean power in dB (which should be about the same for all boards, since transmit power and distance are fixed), and the carrier frequency offset in Hz (the hardware fingerprint, explained in task 2.5). Everything after this task works from that table and never touches the raw files again.

import numpy as np, json, glob, os
import pandas as pd

def load_buffer(d, k):
    """Buffer k of a recording, back to complex numbers."""
    return d[k, 0].astype(np.float32) + 1j*d[k, 1].astype(np.float32)

def find_bursts(x, fs, win=200, k=10, min_len_s=200e-6):
    """Return a list of (start, end) sample indices of bursts in the complex array x."""
    p   = np.abs(x)**2
    env = np.convolve(p, np.ones(win)/win, mode="same")   # smooth over 200 samples = 10 µs
    thr = k * np.median(env)                              # the median is the silence level; bursts are far above it
    on  = env > thr
    edges  = np.diff(on.astype(int))
    starts = np.where(edges ==  1)[0]
    ends   = np.where(edges == -1)[0]
    if len(ends) and len(starts) and ends[0] < starts[0]:
        ends = ends[1:]                                   # a burst already in progress at sample 0 is dropped
    n = min(len(starts), len(ends))
    return [(s, e) for s, e in zip(starts[:n], ends[:n]) if (e - s) > min_len_s*fs]

def cfo_hz(burst, fs, nfft=2**20):
    """Carrier frequency offset of one burst, in Hz, by the squaring method (explained in task 2.5)."""
    y = burst / np.abs(burst).max()
    z = y**2
    Z = np.abs(np.fft.fft(z, nfft))
    f = np.fft.fftfreq(nfft, 1/fs)
    return f[np.argmax(Z)] / 2

rows = []
for day in ["day1", "day2"]:
    for fpath in sorted(glob.glob(f"02_captures/{day}/board*_pos*_day*.npy")):
        if fpath.endswith("_times.npy"):
            continue
        meta  = json.load(open(fpath.replace(".npy", ".json")))
        times = np.load(fpath.replace(".npy", "_times.npy"))
        d  = np.load(fpath); fs = meta["fs_Hz"]
        for k in range(d.shape[0]):
            x = load_buffer(d, k)
            for s, e in find_bursts(x, fs):
                b = x[s:e]
                rows.append(dict(day=day, board=int(meta["board"]), buffer=k, t_s=float(times[k]),
                                 length_us=(e - s)/fs*1e6,
                                 power_dB=10*np.log10(np.mean(np.abs(b)**2)),
                                 cfo_Hz=cfo_hz(b, fs)))
        print(fpath, "bursts so far:", len(rows))

df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
df.to_csv("02_captures/features_per_burst.csv", index=False)
print(df.groupby(["day", "board"]).agg(n=("length_us", "size"),
                                      length_us=("length_us", "median"),
                                      power_dB=("power_dB", "mean"),
                                      cfo_kHz=("cfo_Hz", lambda v: np.median(v)/1e3)).round(1))

What you should see. About eighty bursts per recording, each between 0.8 and 1.2 ms long. The printed summary should show the median length growing by about 16 microseconds from one board to the next, the mean power within a decibel or two across boards, and a CFO of a few kilohertz to a few tens of kilohertz that differs from board to board. Plot the power of one buffer with the detected start and end marked as vertical lines to confirm by eye that the marks sit at the edges of the bumps. A recording reporting zero bursts has the threshold too high or the recording empty; one reporting hundreds has interference or a threshold too low. Change k and look again before changing anything else. This script takes a while on 7 GB of files; run it once per day of captures and keep the CSV.

Task 2.4 The easy classifier first version by 8 Nov with day 1 only, final by 10 Nov with both days

Read. The scikit-learn pages on nearest neighbours classification and on the confusion matrix display. Install with pip install scikit-learn pandas.

What this does. A classifier is a function that takes measured numbers and returns a board number. The simplest one looks up the training bursts closest to the new burst and returns their board. Trained on the day 1 bursts and tested on the day 2 bursts, using only the burst length, it should get almost every burst right, since the ten lengths are 16 microseconds apart and the measurement noise is a microsecond or two. The confusion matrix shows, for each true board, which board the classifier answered, and a correct classifier shows a diagonal. The same code is then run with the mean power as the feature, which should fail, since all boards transmit the same power; that failure is worth a sentence on the slide, because it shows that a feature has to differ between boards and stay the same for one board.

import pandas as pd, numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.metrics import accuracy_score, ConfusionMatrixDisplay

df = pd.read_csv("02_captures/features_per_burst.csv")
train = df[df.day == "day1"]; test = df[df.day == "day2"]     # with day 1 only, use train_test_split instead

def run(feature_cols, title):
    clf = KNeighborsClassifier(n_neighbors=5)
    clf.fit(train[feature_cols].values, train.board.values)
    pred = clf.predict(test[feature_cols].values)
    acc  = accuracy_score(test.board.values, pred)
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6,6))
    ConfusionMatrixDisplay.from_predictions(test.board.values, pred, ax=ax, colorbar=False)
    ax.set_title(f"{title}: accuracy {100*acc:.1f} %")
    fig.savefig(f"04_slides/fig_confusion_{'_'.join(feature_cols)}.png", dpi=200, bbox_inches="tight")
    plt.show()
    return acc

run(["length_us"], "burst length (the announced number)")
run(["power_dB"],  "mean power (should fail)")

plt.figure(figsize=(10,4))
for day, c in [("day1", "#2b6cb0"), ("day2", "#d69e2e")]:
    s = df[df.day == day]
    plt.scatter(s.board + (0.1 if day == "day2" else -0.1), s.length_us, s=6, color=c, label=day)
plt.xticks(range(1, 11)); plt.xlabel("board"); plt.ylabel("burst length (µs)"); plt.legend(); plt.grid(alpha=.3)
plt.savefig("04_slides/fig_length_per_board.png", dpi=200, bbox_inches="tight"); plt.show()

What you should see. For the burst length, a diagonal confusion matrix and an accuracy above 99 percent, and a scatter plot with ten flat clouds climbing in steps of 16 microseconds. For the power, a confusion matrix smeared everywhere and an accuracy near 10 percent, which is chance for ten boards. If the length classifier is also near chance, the burst detector is cutting bursts inconsistently; go back to task 2.3 and look at the marks on a buffer before anything else.

Task 2.5 The real fingerprint: carrier frequency offset as far as time allows, by 10 Nov

Read. The carrier frequency offset discussion in Shen et al. [6], which shows that CFO separates devices well on one day and drifts between days with temperature, so that a classifier relying on it alone degrades. Keep that in mind when you look at your day 2 result. Re-read the I and Q section of Part 1, where the rotation of the clock hand is the CFO.

What the number in the table is. The ESP32 transmits its beacon at 1 Mbit/s using a scheme where the phase of the wave flips by 180 degrees from bit to bit. Squaring the signal removes those flips, since a flip of 180 degrees becomes a flip of 360 degrees, which is no flip at all. What remains after squaring is a pure tone at twice the carrier frequency offset. An FFT of the squared burst therefore has one sharp peak, and its frequency divided by two is the CFO of that burst; that is what cfo_hz in task 2.3 computes. The Pluto's own oscillator adds a constant offset to every board, which is why only the differences between boards matter, and why the Pluto must not be restarted between boards on the same day. Unlike the burst length, nobody programmed this number; it comes from the crystal on each board.

Run. The same notebook as task 2.4, with the CFO as the feature, plus the plot of CFO per board over the two days.

run(["cfo_Hz"], "carrier frequency offset (the hardware fingerprint)")

What you should see. Values of a few kilohertz to a few tens of kilohertz, since a 40 MHz crystal with a tolerance of 10 parts per million puts the 2.4 GHz carrier up to 24 kHz off. For one board on one day the bursts should cluster within a few hundred hertz of each other, with a slow slide during the first minutes as the board warms up. Different boards should sit at different values, so that the plot shows ten small clouds at ten heights. On day 2 each board's cloud should sit near its day 1 cloud but not exactly on it; the shift is the temperature drift Shen et al. describe [6], and it is why the CFO classifier trained on day 1 and tested on day 2 will score below the easy one. If two boards overlap on the same day, that is a true result and it goes on the slide as it is, since it shows why one feature is not enough and why SDP2 adds more features and a neural network. If all boards show the same value, check one burst by hand before anything else.

A sketch of what the plot may look like, not data. Ten boards at ten different offsets, each a tight cloud, with day 2 shifted a little from day 1. Boards 04 and 09 are drawn close on purpose, since the real plot may well have such a pair.
